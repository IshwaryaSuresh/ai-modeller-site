Three practices, one way of working

Start from the drawing you already have. Change the model where it matters, by showing it a reference or saying what you want, and the drawings follow: one change in the model is one change in every sheet.

Architecture

  1. Your DWG in: plans, levels, the staircase drawing
  2. The model, floor by floor, checked against the drawing
  3. Facades and structure edited with pictures: windows, jalis, roofs, rails
  4. Plans, elevations and sections out as DWG
Working today, on a practising studio's house and on Fallingwater from its survey.

Interiors

  1. The plan in, rooms and openings found
  2. Point at a room or a wall, show it a reference
  3. Wardrobes, TV walls, pooja units, ceilings built to it
  4. Renders from the same model
Working today: the model and the reference edits. Interior sheets (elevations, ceiling plans) are next.

Landscape

  1. The site from the drawing: boundary, contours, levels, steps
  2. Water, rock, paving and decks by reference
  3. Step-free routes checked as you go
  4. The ground in every elevation and section
Working today: the site, water and decks. Planting and landscape sheets are next.

Starting from a SketchUp model?

Some institutions begin with a model, not a drawing. Bringing a SketchUp model across as walls, floors and openings you can edit, and drawing it out as DWG, is the next thing being built.

From survey drawings, too

The same process on two landmarks, from the U.S. government's measured surveys: a study model from the survey sheets, and a set drawn from the model.

Fallingwater

Frank Lloyd Wright, 1936–39. Study model from the HABS survey, PA-5346 (Library of Congress; public domain). Not Wright's drawings.

The Farnsworth House

Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, 1945–51. Model from one survey plan, HABS IL-1105 sheet 3 (Library of Congress; public domain). Not Mies's drawings.
